CloudView · UI/UX audit and redesign · 2 October 2026

It is not the gold paint.
It is that nothing on the
screen means anything.

141 screens of the product as built were captured from a disposable instance and read by six specialists against their own failing conditions. They returned 72 findings — 5 blockers, 55 majors, 12 minors, with five causes behind them. Below: the causes, where your own brief was right and where it was not, what is worth keeping, the five screen groups that fix them with the redesigned screens beside the current ones, and six decisions only you can make. Nothing has been applied.

The verdict

Five causes

A product with this many findings usually has a handful of structural decisions behind them. These are CloudView's. Fix these and most of the list goes with them.

01

There is no design system — there is a pile of utility classes

Nothing is defined once. 1,996 rounded-* classes in 30 distinct values; 7 to 10 different corner radii on a single screen, five of them inside a 13-pixel band on the guest guide. 1,481 font-black — on the orders board, 192 elements sit at weight 900 against 184 at weight 400, so the heaviest weight available is the product's default, not its emphasis. 15 to 28 distinct text colours per dashboard screen, including two golds thirteen points apart on one page. No webfont is loaded at all: the whole product renders in the operating system's UI font.

The proof that there are no tokens is dark mode. It is implemented as roughly 180 lines of !important overrides keyed to literal hex values and Tailwind colour classes — .dark .cloudview-dashboard-shell .bg-[#fff8e7] and 60 more like it. That file exists because the components carry no semantic colour to invert.

02

Every screen advertises itself before it works

The orders board says the word Orders four times — nav item, page title, hero "Order Operations Board", list heading — and the concept six times, before the first order. On the analytics screen the largest element is the slogan "A clearer pulse of hotel performance." at 48px, 60% larger than the page's own H1 and exactly twice the size of the revenue figure the screen exists to deliver. The guest menu stacks three headers before the first dish. Four guest screens carry two <h1> elements; on the Dining page both of them are the word "Dining".

The cost is the fold. A guest hunting for the Wi-Fi password reads five headings before reaching the search box that answers it in one tap. On the checkout, the Place Order button sits 2.5 screens below the fold — and the only primary-coloured button above the fold is a loyalty upsell in the identical gold.

03

Colour carries no meaning, so the loudest controls are the dangerous ones

Tints are bound to the card, not the value inside it, so they cannot move when the number does: on the analytics screen four failing metrics are painted green and the one good metric is painted neutral. In the order-details modal three green READY badges sit above an order badged UNPAID in red.

The same confusion decides the buttons. On each order card the two highest-chroma controls are Reject (red) and Mark Paid (green, full width, 2.06× every other button) — the routine action, Accept, is the quiet black one, and in dark mode it renders black-on-black at about 1.1:1, invisible. On the kitchen tablet, Reject cancels a guest's order with one tap, 8 pixels from Accept, with no confirmation at all — while the same action on the desk screen is correctly gated behind a reason modal. Mark Paid moves ₱707.60 with one click, no confirm, no amount restated, on four visually interchangeable cards.

04

The navigation and the vocabulary belong to the database, not to the hotel

Nineteen sidebar items named for code modules — the nav literally reads "Services Module", and the guide page's own H1 is "Hotel Guide Module". Setting up one chargeable towel service touches three items in two groups. The guest's four tabs reach 4 of 16 screens; the other twelve hang off links inside other screens, seven behind a tab called "Profile" — and the product ships an FAQ whose first three entries are navigation instructions for itself.

The words are worse than the structure. One order lifecycle is told in four different vocabularies — the guest is shown "PENDING", then "Waiting for confirmation", while the board says "Needs acceptance" and the kitchen calls READY "Serving". Seven different strings mean "has not paid yet". The guide's content unit is called an item, a card, content, a detail and "1 ITEMS" — four of those on one screen. The thing a guest asks for has six names, and the interface hedges with a slash rather than choosing: "Create Service / Add-on", "CUSTOMER / GUEST NAME", "Track / Manage". The sticker on the wall is an NFC Tag, an access point, a launch link, a destination, a location and a Panel. And raw enums reach guests directly: a guest's own order carries the chip "CAN CANCEL ITEMS" — a boolean permission flag upcased — and their activity screen counts "XENDIT 0", a payment gateway's company name offered to someone on holiday as a category of their own spending. The same guest is "Maria Santos" on one screen and "Guest" two taps later; the hotel is "Cloud View Demo Hotel" on four screens and "Cloud View Resort & Hotel" on the one they stare at while waiting for food.

05

The empty and broken cases were never drawn

The source holds 56 distinct empty-state strings in two incompatible vocabularies — "No X found" and "No X yet" — and the blaming one is used exactly where nothing was filtered. A guest tapping Request service, one of two primary tiles on the arrival screen, is told "No services found. Try another category or clear your search." There is no other category, no search term, and no clear control on the page: the hotel simply has no services configured. Both named recoveries are impossible. On a fresh install that is the hotel's second-most-used function, dead, with no fallback — no front-desk number, no way back.

There is no not-found.tsx and no error.tsx anywhere in the repository, so a guest who submits a service request lands on a bare white Next.js 404 — system font, no hotel name, no navigation. The room-passcode screen says "No active guest stay was found for this room. Please contact the front desk." and then offers a fully enabled form and a gold "Authorize Device" button. Three of the hotel's four staff accounts report "0 visible modules · Needs save to repair landing" beside a green "Active" pill, in developer shorthand, with no repair control.

Before anything is changed

What is kept

A critique that finds nothing to keep is not credible. Every lens was asked what should survive, and these came back. Several are better than anything the redesign would have invented.

  • Order tracking (g06) handles waiting properly. A named status, a bounded estimate, a five-step rail, a live timer, an "updated" stamp and a human escape hatch. Across all 141 captures no lens could find a wait with nothing on screen — the product consistently swaps buttons to "Submitting…", applies optimistic updates and raises a toast. That discipline survives untouched.
  • The guest guide's type ramp already works. The same content the staff screen renders with 98 weight-900 elements and 25 text colours, the guest screen renders with one element at 900 and a six-step weight ramp. The redesign generalises this ramp; it did not invent it.
  • The kitchen's three lanes are the job. Pending / Preparing / Serving with the count in the lane head is the only place in the product where a hue maps to a state that actually changes. The orders board should move toward the kitchen, not the reverse. And the correct sizing already exists in the code — TV Mode raises the order code to 30px and the dish lines to 16px. It is behind an opt-in button.
  • Two zero-states that resolve themselves. The staff services screen offers "Add Default Services" — one click from nothing to a working catalogue — and the backups screen says "No backups yet. Create the first full hotel backup above." Those two are the template for the other 54 strings.
  • The guest requests screen is the best empty state in the product: icon, serif headline, one sentence saying exactly when content appears, one action, all inside a single phone screen.
  • The POS total block is the one place the most important number is the largest. Food, services, subtotal in regular; Total largest and heaviest at the bottom. It is the model the stat cards are not following.
  • Destructive actions are already protected in two of the four places they occur — the desk's Reject demands a reason, and the guest's item cancellation is a two-step disclosure. The fix is extending those two, not inventing a pattern.
  • The access-denied screen is the one error that reads as designed: a lock, the reason, and a literal instruction for what to do next.
  • The guide section page is the only screen offering lateral movement — "Continue exploring" with its three sibling sections. Every other leaf is a cul-de-sac.
  • All five palettes, next-themes and ThemePaletteProvider stay. So does every route, every data flow and every real string of copy.
The work

Five groups, ordered by what they fix

Grouped by cause rather than by menu, so the screens in each group agree with one another when they land.

Group A

The system itself

Tokens, the six ui/ primitives, the shell, the sidebar and the bottom bar. Nothing else can be fixed before this: radius, colour, type, spacing and hairlines get defined once in globals.css and tailwind.config.ts, every component ships its eight states, and the 180 lines of !important dark-mode overrides are deleted because the components finally carry semantic colour. The primitives keep their public API, so the 121 files that use them are a class sweep, not a rewrite.

Resolves VH-1, VH-5, VH-6, VH-7, VH-8, VH-13, IX-3, A11Y-1, A11Y-4, A11Y-6, A11Y-10, A11Y-11, and ST-7's 56 empty-state strings. One accent action per screen; the gold becomes a 1px rule; status becomes a dot and a word; focus is a 2px outline at 2px offset that survives the loss of radius.

Group B

The guest spine — arrival, menu, checkout

The three screens a guest actually uses, in order. The arrival screen becomes the key-card moment: the property's photograph full-bleed and square-cornered, its name in the display serif, and three numbered actions on hairlines — not a grid of coloured tiles with the guest's own name set larger than anything they came to do. The menu becomes an editorial list. The checkout becomes a printed receipt, with the two facts the hotel already knows shown as facts rather than empty required fields.

Resolves VH-2, VH-11, VH-13, IX-4, IX-5, IX-6, IX-7, IX-10, CP-1, CP-3. The guest reaches the first dish in one screen instead of three headers; the order total is the largest thing on the checkout and the button that places it names the amount; quantity controls go from 32px to 44px; the only destructive control on the screen stops being a twin of the back button; the single error banner becomes an error at the field it is about. The dish name moves off the photograph onto the surface beside a fixed square thumbnail, which is what takes A11Y-2 from 1.00:1 to the body ramp.

Group C

The staff console

The orders board, the kitchen display, the POS and the order detail. One header per screen; figures as figure blocks with a rule above and a serif numeral; a hairline table instead of cards-inside-cards; one emphasised action per row with the destructive one moved into the record it belongs to. The kitchen is the deliberate exception — it keeps its scale and its contrast and takes only the corners, the hairlines and the typeface.

Resolves VH-1, VH-2, VH-3, VH-4, VH-5, VH-9, VH-10, IX-1 (blocker), IX-2 (blocker), IX-3, IX-9, IX-11, IA-4, A11Y-7, A11Y-8, A11Y-9. On the kitchen ticket the dish and the timer become the largest things on the screen, a cancelled line is struck through and worded rather than dotted, and the one-tap cancel is gone. On the board, ₱0.00 stops being the biggest number on the page.

Group D

The states, and the dead ends

Spans twelve screens but is one piece of work: one empty-state vocabulary instead of 56 strings in two, written like a concierge note; the blaming wording used only where something was actually filtered; a not-found.tsx and an error.tsx that keep the guest inside the hotel's portal; a skeleton that belongs to the region loading rather than replacing the whole app including its navigation; and the two screens that already resolve their own emptiness used as the template for the rest.

Resolves ST-1 (blocker), ST-2, ST-3, ST-4, ST-5, ST-6, ST-7, ST-8, ST-9, IX-9. The highest-value single change in the whole audit is here: on a fresh install the guest's second-most-used function currently dead-ends, and the fix is copy plus one fallback action.

Group E

Navigation and vocabulary

The sidebar regrouped by what staff do rather than by database module; the guest's four tabs made to reach what a guest actually needs; and one word per concept across both surfaces. This group changes shape, so all of it is in the decisions below and none of it moves without your answer.

Resolves IA-1, IA-2, IA-3, IA-5, IA-6, IA-7, IA-9, IA-10, IA-11, IA-12, CP-2. The login is included here because it is the one screen whose whole job is identity: today it opens with "Welcome back" over a gradient and three feature tiles.

Yours to decide

Six decisions

Each of these changes the shape of the product, not just its surface. Nothing in group E starts until they are answered.

1 · The typeface pair

The product loads no webfont today, which is half of the cheapness. The mockups use EB Garamond for display, figures and prices, and Instrument Sans for interface text.

EB Garamond + Instrument Sans — recommended. Garamond has the old-style figures and the quiet authority of printed stationery; Instrument Sans is a neutral grotesque with real tabular numerals and no SaaS association.
EB Garamond + Archivo. Slightly narrower and more editorial; better at very small tracked caps, marginally heavier on screen.
Cormorant Garamond + Instrument Sans. Finer, more fashion-house, noticeably weaker below 18px — a risk on a phone in sunlight.

2 · Regrouping the dashboard, and three renames

Nineteen items in five groups named after parts of the system. The proposal groups them by when they are used — Today (overview, orders, kitchen, service requests, counter sales), The hotel (stays, rooms, panels, guide), What we sell (menu, services, stock, rewards), Records (reports, settings) — and renames "Services Module" → Services, "POS Terminal" → Counter sales, "Hotel Guide Module" → Hotel guide.

Regroup and rename — recommended. Staff vocabulary is a content decision, so it is yours, not the redesign's.
Regroup, keep the names. Safer if staff have learned them.

3 · The guest's four tabs, and two screens that are the same list

Four tabs cannot represent sixteen screens, and today they reach four of them while telling a guest they are on "Home" when they are reading "My Requests". /orders and /activity show the same order with the guest named two different ways — "Guest" on one, "Maria Santos" on the other.

Merge /orders and /activity into one "My stay" screen, and rename the fourth tab from "Profile" to My stay — recommended. One place for everything a guest has going on; the tab stops naming a database record.
Keep both screens, cross-link them and fix the active tab. Less disruption, keeps two answers to one question.

4 · Does the guest portal follow the theme?

It does not today — there is not one dark: style anywhere under src/app/t/, so the portal is permanently a near-black field and the theme toggle does nothing there. Two of the captured guest screens are byte-identical in light and dark.

Keep it permanently dark, and say so — recommended. The dark field is the key-card look and it is what the photography needs. Make it a deliberate rule rather than an accident, and fix the contrast inside it.
Make it follow the theme. A real light mode for the portal is better in bright sunlight, and is roughly a group of its own in cost.

5 · Make the kitchen's TV Mode the default

The correct sizing for a two-metre read already exists in src/app/dashboard/kitchen/page.tsx, behind one of six equally weighted chrome buttons. The captured default sets the order code at 14px and the dish lines at 12px, while the words "Kitchen Display" are 30px.

Default to TV Mode on that route; keep a "desk view" toggle — recommended.
Leave it opt-in. Nothing changes for anyone who has learned the button.

6 · Is the public landing page in scope?

/ is 11,770px tall and carries 32 blurred elements, 65 gradient backgrounds, 8 glow shadows and 14 corner radii. Its hierarchy below the fold depends entirely on a scroll-triggered opacity reveal: measured in the static render, a section heading sits at about 1.6:1 against its background, and eighteen of nineteen headings are at or near background luminance. It is a marketing site, not the product.

Out of scope for now — recommended. It sells to hotel owners; the product serves guests and staff. Fix it separately.
In scope. Adds roughly one group, and it is the first thing a prospective owner sees.
